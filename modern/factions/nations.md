# The Nations

> Two hundred states woke up to the same war. They agreed on almost nothing except that it was not a war any of them could fight alone.

## The Charter

On **14 February 2032**, the nations of Earth chartered **Joint Portal Command** — not a treaty organization and not a world government, but a command, with one mandate: no gate unguarded, no gate unaccounted for. The deal that made it possible was simple, and every capital understood it as a bargain rather than a surrender:

- **Jurisdiction for protection.** A member nation cedes operational command over any gate on its territory, and gets in return a seat, a voice, and the guarantee that no gate fights alone.
- **No national gates.** No national force operates a gate without JPC's colors; no national laboratory studies an anchor without JPC's oversight. This was the clause the charter was built around — the alternative was seventeen national armies pointing guns at the same nine doorways.
- **The gates are a shared asset.** Whatever the nations learn about the anchors, the far side, or the war belongs to the command, not to any one flag.

The charter was signed by most of the world in the first year, and by almost all of it since. "Most" and "almost" are doing the work in that sentence, and they always have.

## The Gate Hosts

Nine gates survived the Scouring. Six of the nine stand on the territory of a member state, and those six states carry the war's strangest burden: a foreign garrison, permanently, on their own soil, guarding a door they could not destroy and were not allowed to keep.

| Nation | Gate | Posture |
|--------|------|---------|
| **Russia** | The Ural Scar (destroyed) | Charter member, unquiet. Nuked its own gate rather than lose it; the Scar is sovereign territory and national memory. Its generals never forgave the charter for what the March cost them — and never stopped arguing that Russia saved the world first. |
| **Norway** | Gate Alpha (held) | Charter member, host. The horde walked ashore on its coast; it hosts the busiest node in the war and the prestige garrison. The most pro-JPC state in Europe, and the one with the least choice in the matter. |
| **Iran** | The Zagros Gate (contained) | Charter member, resentful. Hosts an unbreakable gate in its own mountains; "politics forbid the yield" is JPC's polite way of saying Tehran has a veto over its own destruction. The status-of-forces agreement is renegotiated annually and expires every December, on purpose. |
| **Saudi Arabia** | The Dune Gate (contained) | Charter member, quiet. Hosts the gate under the sand, pays for logistics, and has never once commented on it publicly. The garrison's standing joke is that the kingdom's official position on the Reopening is "no comment". |
| **DR Congo** | The Jungle Gate (contained) | Charter member, strained. The JPC garrison is the largest armed presence in the country; Kinshasa resents it, depends on it, and has twice demanded its size be cut. The gate's dispersed horde is still in the forest, which nobody in either capital likes to say out loud. |
| **Peru** | The High Gate (contained) | Charter member, minor. A window in a cliff at 4,700 m; contributes mountaineers, radio operators, and the country's highest permanent military post. The garrison is small, the mysticism is local, and the rope work is world-class. |
| **Denmark (Greenland)** | The Ice Gate (contained) | Charter member, outmatched. A small state hosting an unreachable gate under its own ice. The garrison answers to JPC, the territory to Copenhagen, and the ice to no one. Denmark's contribution is disproportionately large for its size, which is its quiet way of proving it is not afraid. |

Three more gates survive on ground no member state can claim: the White Gate in Antarctica, watched by the coldest rotation in JPC; and the two held-open doors, whose far sides are the only ground the charter governs absolutely. The nations that host a contained gate tend to resent the charter; the nations that host nothing tend to envy the hosts, which is the argument nobody ever finishes.

## The Holdouts

Not every state signed. The holdouts are few, and they are not the states anyone predicted:

- **India** refused the charter outright — no ceded jurisdiction, no foreign garrison on its soil. The position is easier than it sounds because India fought its own gates in the Scouring and destroyed them, so by February 2032 there was nothing on its territory for JPC to command. New Delhi calls the refusal sovereignty; JPC calls it luck, and says so in briefings where the word is carefully not recorded.
- **A bloc of small states** — island nations and old neutrals — declined the charter's jurisdiction clause while quietly accepting everything else: JPC overflights, JPC ports, JPC logistics. Their objection was never the war; it was the word "command".
- **One member state's history is a lie.** The 2031 siege of a small town is still listed as a "training exercise" in that nation's official records — the town, the horde, and the week it burned are all absent from the public archive. The file exists. The archives are sealed. Every JPC officer who has seen the real records uses the phrase "the training exercise" the way other people use a swear word.

The holdouts matter out of all proportion to their number. Every refusal is a hole in the charter's claim that the gates are a shared asset — and the lords, who read the same newspapers JPC's analysts do, know exactly where the holes are.

## The Unilateral Programs

JPC's third rule — **nothing unilateral** — exists because three member nations tried, and the results are classified. What is known is thin and does not improve with retelling:

- One nation drilled a containment gate's perimeter from outside the exclusion zone, on the theory that a second approach was a second option.
- One ran unapproved experiments on anchor fragments recovered from a destroyed site, on the theory that the frame could be studied without the doorway.
- One funded a private crossing attempt — through a gate, without the command's colors, with a crew that was never named.

None of the three produced anything JPC wanted. All three produced something JPC has spent years making sure the lords never hear about. The nations are not named; the charter gives JPC the power to audit any member and the wisdom never to say which.

## For Story Use

### Themes to Explore
- **Sovereignty vs. survival** — the hardest war ever fought is also the first one fought by committee.
- **The politics of shared weapons** — nine doors, two hundred states, one command, and a thousand arguments about who owns what.
- **What nations hide during an apocalypse** — the record of the Reopening is not one history; it is two hundred of them, and they disagree.
- **The cost of being a host** — the states that carry the garrisons carry the war differently from everyone else.

### Story Hooks
- The Zagros status-of-forces agreement expires in December, and for the first time the vote to renew it is expected to fail — which would make the unbreakable gate the world's first ungoverned one.
- A holdout state requests observer status at JPC after a gate-class anchor is found on its continental shelf — the anchor is dormant, and the holdout's government has known about it since 2032.
- The "training exercise" archives are declassified by accident, and the records show the town was evacuated, bombed, and erased from the map in the same week — with the evacuation logged before the bombing, and both before the horde arrived.
- One of the three unilateral programs is exposed: a member nation has been running a gate program under a civilian agency's budget line for two years, and the line item has a name.
- A gate host state threatens to withdraw from the charter and garrison its own gate alone — and Mensah's response, delivered in one line, becomes the thing the command repeats for a generation.
